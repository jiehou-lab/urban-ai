## Learning objectives

By the end of this class you will be able to:

1. Locate bias at each stage: problem framing, data collection, labeling, modeling, deployment, and interpretation.
2. Apply a privacy and data-minimization check to an urban dataset.
3. State what transparency requires for a system a resident can contest.
4. Connect the course's practices to MSU's generative AI guidelines and to professional planning ethics.

## Session outline

**Bias is not one thing.** Six places it enters, with an urban example for each.

**Equity in outcomes, not just in code.** Whose error rate, whose wait time, whose neighborhood gets the intervention.

**Privacy and surveillance in public space.** Aggregation, re-identification, consent that no one gave, and data minimization as a default.

**Transparency and contestability.** Being able to tell a resident what decided their case, and giving them a way to challenge it.

**Accountability.** Who is responsible when the system is wrong: procurement, oversight, and the named human.

**Participation as a safeguard.** Involving affected communities early enough that the framing itself can still change.


## In-class activity

**Discussion Forum B — *Whose city does the model see?*** Post one place in your team's own workflow where a group was under-represented, and the specific change you made or would make.

## What comes next

Final team presentations, reflection, and post-survey close the course.
