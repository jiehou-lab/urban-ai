## 1. Course description

Cities decide things constantly — where a bus line goes, which buildings get inspected, whose flood risk gets
funded — and those decisions increasingly pass through AI systems. Urban AI introduces AI-driven decision
support systems (DSSs) in the context of real urban development and planning challenges.

Students explore the concept of Urban AI, the history and evolution of decision support systems, and the
application of AI in advanced decision-support workflows. The course introduces technologies now used in
planning practice: computer vision for street-view analysis, text mining for geolocated social media and public
comment, clustering and classification for neighborhood analysis, scenario simulation, and large language
models for stakeholder engagement.

Working in interdisciplinary teams, students use approved AI decision-support tools to process information,
analyze sites, test planning scenarios, and develop recommendations. The course emphasizes responsible AI use,
human supervision, transparency, equity, and stakeholder-centered decision-making. Every team completes an
**Urban AI Decision-Support Brief** demonstrating the ability to use AI critically, ethically, and creatively on
a real urban challenge.

The course is built so that a student who has never written a line of code can complete every activity.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

1. **Define** Urban AI and AI-driven decision support systems, and explain how they support evidence-based
   urban development decisions.
2. **Describe** typical decision-making processes in planning — problem framing, stakeholder identification,
   decision criteria, alternative development, tradeoff analysis, and human judgment.
3. **Use** approved AI tools responsibly to support urban analytic tasks such as processing information,
   comparing alternatives, and engaging stakeholders.
4. **Evaluate** AI-generated outputs for accuracy, bias, uncertainty, missing context, privacy risks, and
   relevance to a specific urban issue.
5. **Develop** a human-centered decision-support workflow that includes stakeholder input, transparent
   assumptions, ethical safeguards, and human review.
6. **Produce** a practical Urban AI Decision-Support Brief demonstrating critical, ethical, and creative AI use
   in a real-world urban context.

## 6. Labs: two tracks, one artifact

Every lab runs two ways. Students choose either, and may switch between them at any point. Both produce the
same submitted artifact.

- **Track A — No code (default).** Web tools and worksheet templates. No installation, no Python.
- **Track B — Colab (go deeper).** A pre-filled Google Colab notebook. Students run cells and change clearly
  marked `# ▶ CHANGE ME` parameters, then answer interpretation questions. No code is written from scratch.

Track B notebooks run offline with synthetic-but-realistic urban data generated in-notebook under a fixed seed,
so every student sees the same results and no lab depends on an external service staying online. Instructor
notes in each notebook name the real open-data sources to substitute.

| Lab | Title | Artifact | Built-in responsible-AI moment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | AI in the browser + first Colab | AI Use Log entry #1 | An unsupportable claim you must log |
| 1 | Text mining community voice | Top-themes table + bias note | Comment rates per capita differ ~2× by neighborhood |
| 2 | Clustering analysis | Neighborhood typology + k justification | Cluster membership shifts as k changes |
| 3 | Time series analysis | Trend brief + forecast caveat | A structural break breaks the forecast |
| 4 | Classification | Subgroup error table + fairness note | Error rate roughly 2× higher for low-income tracts |
| 5 | Risk-response DSS | Ranked alternatives + sensitivity chart | Ranking flips under a small weight change |
| 6 | DSS AI lab | **D4** Scenario Comparison Matrix | Different weight sets produce different winners |
| 7 | LLM lab | Grounded memo + hallucination log | Half the test questions are unanswerable from the source |

Every lab closes with the same four reflection prompts:

1. What did the tool assume?
2. Who is missing from this data?
3. What would change your recommendation?
4. What must a human verify before this is used?

### The final brief

Required sections: the urban issue; site analysis; stakeholders; decision criteria and weights; scenario
comparison (at least two scenarios); AI output evaluation; responsible AI review; human oversight plan;
recommendation; AI use disclosure. Target length 8–12 pages including figures.

Two requirements are non-negotiable. Every factual claim must be traceable to a named source a reader could
check. And the brief must show at least one point where human judgment overrode or corrected the AI.

## 9. Responsible AI policy

1. **Approved tools only.** Use the MSU-approved AI tools listed in D2L. Any new AI tool, subscription, or LMS
   integration goes through MSU IT review for feasibility, security, FERPA compliance, and accessibility first.
2. **Keep the AI Use Log.** Date, tool, task, prompt or input, output, what you changed, what you verified it
   against. The log is a graded part of workflow documentation.
3. **AI may draft; humans decide.** Every recommendation submitted is the student's, and the student defends it.
4. **Everything must be checkable.** Any claim you cannot verify against a named source gets verified or cut.
   Fluent text is not evidence.
5. **No personal or restricted data.** Public or synthetic data only. Nothing identifiable, nothing under FERPA,
   nothing under an NDA, nothing collected for a different purpose.
6. **Disclosure is never penalized; concealment is.** Presenting unverified AI output as your own work violates
   this agreement and MSU academic integrity policy.

The course aligns with the MSU Guidelines for the Use of Generative AI and, for planning students, with the
AICP Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct.

## 10. Accessibility and inclusive design

The course is structured along Universal Design for Learning principles and a Quality Matters-informed online
structure, developed with instructional design and accessibility support.

- Every session is available live and as a captioned recording.
- All documents use real heading structure and are screen-reader navigable; figures are described in text.
- No activity requires a specific device, paid software, or a coding environment.
- Labs offer two paths at every step so students can work at the depth that fits them.
- Students needing accommodations should contact the MSU Resource Center for Persons with Disabilities and the
  instructional team; arrangements are made before the cohort begins wherever possible.

## 12. Course policies

**Attendance.** Meetings are synchronous and interactive. Missing more than one meeting jeopardizes completion;
contact the instructional team in advance if a conflict arises and a make-up path will be arranged.

**Teams.** Teams of 3–5 form at Meeting 3 and stay together through the final brief. Teams are built to be
interdisciplinary wherever the cohort composition allows.

**Late work.** Weekly mini-tasks accepted until the following meeting. Deliverables 3–5 anchor the team's
downstream work; extensions require prior arrangement.

**Communication.** Course announcements and questions go through D2L. Expect a response within one business day.

**Feedback.** Students are surveyed at the end of every cohort, and materials are revised on a rolling basis.
Completing students are eligible to apply as student facilitators for future cohorts.

## 13. Sustainability and scale

Urban AI is designed for repeated online cohorts — approximately 10–12 cohorts of 42–50 students per year,
reaching 500+ students annually. Scale is supported by reusable case materials, a reusable D2L master shell,
and trained student facilitators rather than repeated redevelopment. The School of Planning, Design and
Construction has committed to sustaining the experience for at least three academic years.
