Every student signs the [Responsible AI Agreement]({{ '/assets/templates/Responsible_AI_Agreement.docx' | relative_url }})
before Meeting 2. This page is that agreement in plain language.

## 1. Use approved tools only

Use the MSU-approved AI tools listed in the course D2L shell. If you want to use something else, ask first —
any new AI tool, subscription, or LMS integration goes through MSU IT review for feasibility, security, FERPA
compliance, and accessibility before it can be used for coursework.

## 2. Keep the AI Use Log

Every time an AI tool contributes to work you submit, log it: date, tool, the task, the prompt or input you
gave it, what the output was, **what you changed**, and **what you verified it against**. The log is a graded
part of your workflow documentation.

The log is not a confession. It is a lab notebook. Professionals keep one.

## 3. AI may draft. Humans decide.

An AI system in this course can summarize, structure, suggest, and compare. It cannot decide. Every
recommendation you submit is yours, and you are the person who has to defend it.

## 4. Everything must be checkable

Every factual claim in a submitted deliverable must be traceable to a source a reader could open. If the AI
gave you a number, a citation, or a claim you cannot verify, you either verify it or you cut it. Fluent text is
not evidence.

## 5. No personal or restricted data

Use public or synthetic data only. Do not paste into an AI tool: student records, identifiable individuals,
health information, anything under FERPA, anything under an NDA, or a dataset you were given for a different
purpose. When in doubt, aggregate first or ask the instructor.

## 6. Disclosure is never penalized. Concealment is.

Telling us that AI wrote a first draft costs you nothing. Presenting AI output as your own verified work, or
submitting claims you did not check, is a violation of this agreement and of MSU academic integrity policy.

## 7. Accessibility and equity in your own work

Your final brief will be read by people who are not in this course. Write so a non-technical stakeholder can
follow it. Use real headings, describe your figures in text, and do not rely on color alone to carry meaning.

## Why these rules exist

AI in urban decision-making carries specific, documented risks: biased training data reproducing historical
patterns of disinvestment; privacy erosion when public-space data is aggregated and re-identified; opaque
systems that residents cannot contest; and automation bias, where a plausible-looking output stops the human
review that was supposed to catch it.

None of these are solved by a policy page. They are managed by habits — logging, verifying, checking who is
missing from the data, and asking what would change the recommendation. Those habits are what the course
actually teaches. The deliverables are the scaffolding that builds them.
